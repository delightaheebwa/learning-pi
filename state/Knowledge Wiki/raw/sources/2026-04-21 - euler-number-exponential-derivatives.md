## Extracted notes

- For a general base \(a\), the derivative of \(a^t\) is proportional to the function itself.
- The proportionality constant depends on the base.
- Examples shown in the screenshots:
  - \(\frac{d}{dt} 2^t \approx 0.693147... \cdot 2^t\)
  - \(\frac{d}{dt} 3^t \approx 1.0986... \cdot 3^t\)
  - \(\frac{d}{dt} 5^t \approx 1.6094... \cdot 5^t\)
  - \(\frac{d}{dt} 7^t \approx 1.9459... \cdot 7^t\)
  - \(\frac{d}{dt} 42^t \approx 0.18545... \cdot 42^t\)
- The special case is \(e\):
  - \(\frac{d}{dt} e^t = e^t\)
  - So the exponential is equal to its own derivative.
- A base \(a\) can be rewritten as \(a^t = e^{\ln(a)t}\).
- Therefore, \(\frac{d}{dt} a^t = \ln(a)\,a^t\).
- The natural logarithm is the conversion factor that tells you the derivative constant for a given exponential base.
- This same proportionality pattern appears in Newton's law of cooling:
  - \(\frac{d\Delta T}{dt} = -k\Delta T\)
  - \(\Delta T(t) = e^{-kt}\)
