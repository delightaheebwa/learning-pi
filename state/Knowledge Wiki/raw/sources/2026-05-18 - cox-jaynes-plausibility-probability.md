# Cox-Jaynes view: plausibility → probability

## Raw source

Boolean logic is too coarse for everyday plausible reasoning because it only allows strict true/false. In practice we adjust degrees of belief, reweighting hypotheses when we see new evidence. If we represent these degrees of plausibility with real numbers and demand that they behave rationally and consistently (in the sense of Cox–Jaynes), then the resulting rules are, essentially, exactly the usual rules of probability. Cox–Jaynes view: plausibility → probability

Cox and Jaynes start from the idea: “Let’s assign real numbers to plausibilities and require that this assignment behave in a rational way.” They impose criteria like:

Use real numbers to represent degrees of plausibility.

Combine them in ways that agree with common sense (e.g., evidence supporting a hypothesis should not lower its plausibility).

Be consistent (no contradictions, use all available information, same information pattern ⇒ same plausibility numbers).

Under these conditions, they prove:

Any such plausibility calculus must obey the same functional relationships as standard probability (sum rule, product rule), up to a monotone re-labeling of the scale.

In other words, once you accept those rationality requirements, you have essentially committed yourself to using probability theory (possibly under a different numeric “gauge,” like log-odds instead of raw probabilities). 1. The degrees of plausibility are represented by real numbers. 2. These numbers must be based on the rules of common sense. 3. The resulting reasoning must be consistent, with the three following meanings of the word “consistent”: (a) Consistency or non-contradiction: When the same result can be reached through different means, the same plausibility value must be found in all cases. (b) Honesty: All available data must be taken into account. (c) Reproducibility: If our state of knowledge about two problems are the same, then we must assign the same degree of plausibility to both of them.
