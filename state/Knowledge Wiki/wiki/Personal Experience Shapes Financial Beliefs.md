# Personal Experience Shapes Financial Beliefs

**Insight:** the slice of history you personally lived through is a vanishingly small sample of what has happened in the world, yet it supplies most of what you believe about how money works — so equally smart people disagree in good faith.

## The asymmetry

- Housel's Introduction: *"Your personal experiences with money make up maybe 0.00000001% of what's happened in the world, but maybe 80% of how you think the world works."*
- Two numbers, wildly out of proportion: the **evidence base** for a money worldview is roughly nothing, and the **weight** it carries is roughly everything. Personal experience shapes beliefs, goals and forecasts.
- Read the *"maybe"* as rhetorical, not as a measurement — the claim is the disproportion, not the exact percentages.

## What the disproportion explains

- **Equally smart people disagree** about recessions, investing and risk *because* their experiences differ, not because one of them is reasoning badly. Each worldview is persuasive from the inside, since each is built from a real, lived sample.
- **Generational and cohort splits** fall out of it: a person whose formative money years were inflation, a crash, or a bull market carries a different prior about what "normal" is — and each prior is rational *given its sample*.
- **Disagreement is a difference of priors, not of intelligence**, which is why arguing from a chart rarely moves anyone: the chart is not the other person's experience.

## Behaviour is story-driven — the mechanism that joins the edges

- *"every financial decision a person makes, makes sense to them in that moment and checks the boxes they need to check. They tell themselves a story about what they're doing and why they're doing it, and that story has been shaped by their own unique experiences."*
- That closes the chain `experience → behaviour → outcomes`: behaviour is not an application of knowledge, it is the **enactment of a story**, and the story's raw material is the lived sample. This is the mechanism behind [[Psychology of Money (soft skill)]]'s claim that knowledge does not guarantee behaviour.
- Consequence worth keeping: a decision that looks irrational from outside is usually *coherent* with an experience the observer did not have. The failure to see that is a failure of imagination, not of arithmetic.

## What this is not

- **Not** "experience is right." A narrow or unrepresentative sample produces confidently wrong beliefs — a person whose entire investing window was a bull market has as much reason to think risk is low as a survivor of a crash has to think it is high. The claim is about **where beliefs come from**, not about whether they are true.
- **Not** "data is useless." It says experience is the **default prior**; the correction is deliberate exposure to evidence outside one's own window (history, base rates, other people's samples) — which is exactly what the small-sample problem requires, and exactly what is hard to do because the prior feels like common sense.
- **Nearest-neighbour contrast — base-rate reasoning:** base-rate reasoning asks *what usually happens across all cases*; the lived sample answers *what happened to me*. They agree often enough that the substitution goes unnoticed, and diverge exactly where the personal window is atypical.
- **Failure regime:** the bias is invisible when the lived sample and the base rate agree, and it is *load-bearing* when they don't — that is where no amount of extra information lands, because the belief was never formed from information in the first place.
